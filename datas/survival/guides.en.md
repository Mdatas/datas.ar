# Offline survival — Datas' own guides

Short, practical guides for when the power, the water or the internet fail — for a few hours,
for weeks, or while far from home. Written for this app in plain words; the figures come from
the public sources named at the end of each section. They do not replace the instructions of
your local authorities, emergency services or a doctor: when those can be reached, follow them.

Nobody gets through a long crisis alone. Every section ends with what to do **together**.

## The first hours
<!-- id: start -->

1. **Safety first.** Get away from immediate danger (fire, gas smell, rising water, a building
   that may fall). If there is a gas smell, do not switch lights or anything electric on or off;
   leave and warn others.
2. **Count people, not things.** Who is with you, who is missing, who needs help now (injured,
   children, older people, people with disabilities, pets).
3. **Get information.** A battery or hand-crank radio keeps working when phones and internet do
   not. Listen for official instructions: stay put, or leave.
4. **Save power.** Turn the phone's power-saving mode on, lower the screen, close what you do not
   need. Send short text messages instead of calls; they need less of the network.
5. **Water and warmth before food.** People last a long time without food, much less without
   water or in the cold.
6. **Write things down on paper**: phone numbers, where everyone is, the medicines each person
   takes. Paper works with no battery.

Be ready to look after yourselves for **at least three days** — help may take that long to
reach everyone. A basic kit for each person: water, food that needs no cooking, a light, a
radio, spare batteries or a charged power bank, first-aid supplies, the medicines you take,
copies of documents, some cash, warm clothes.

**Together:** knock on your neighbours' doors in the first hours, especially people who live
alone. Agree on one meeting point for your building or block, and one place where messages are
left on paper.

Sources: FEMA, *Are You Ready?* (the library below; ready.gov/collection/are-you-ready).

## Water
<!-- id: water -->

**How much.** Store at least about **4 litres per person per day** (one US gallon) — more in hot
weather, for people who are sick, pregnant or nursing, and for pets. For drinking and preparing
food alone, about **5 litres per person per day** is a common planning figure in emergencies.

**Making water safe** (when tap water may be unsafe and there is no bottled water):

- **Cloudy water first**: let it settle, or filter it through a clean cloth, paper towel or
  coffee filter, and take the clear water.
- **Boil it**: a rolling boil for **1 minute** (**3 minutes** above about 2,000 m / 6,500 ft).
  Let it cool and keep it in clean, covered containers. Boiling is the surest method.
- **Or use bleach** if you cannot boil: plain, **unscented** household bleach (sodium
  hypochlorite, 5–9 %). About **2 drops per litre** of clear water (8 drops per US gallon);
  **double it** if the water is cloudy. Mix and **wait at least 30 minutes**.
- **Filters**: most portable filters do **not** remove viruses or bacteria; look for one labelled
  for that, or disinfect after filtering.
- Water polluted with **chemicals** (fuel, industrial spills, flood water from factories) is not
  made safe by boiling or bleach. Find another source.

**Hygiene keeps people alive in a long crisis**: wash hands with soap after the toilet and
before food; keep drinking water away from washing water; dig toilets away and downhill from
wells and streams.

**Together:** one clean water point that everyone keeps clean is better than each family on its
own. Agree who tests and treats it, who carries water for those who cannot, and where toilets go.
The WHO technical notes in the library explain cleaning wells and tanks for a whole community.

Sources: CDC, *Make Water Safe During an Emergency*
(cdc.gov/water-emergency/communication-resources/);
CDC, *Personal Preparation and Storage of Safe Water*
(archived copy, webarchive.library.unt.edu);
WHO/WEDC, *Technical Note 5* (repository.lboro.ac.uk).

## Food
<!-- id: food -->

- Keep food that needs **no fridge and little or no cooking**: canned food (and a manual can
  opener), dry pasta and rice, legumes, oats, nuts, dried fruit, crackers, powdered milk.
- **Rotate it**: eat the oldest first and replace it, so nothing expires.
- **When the power goes out**: keep the fridge and freezer **closed**. A fridge keeps food safe
  for about **4 hours**; a **full** freezer about **48 hours**, a half-full one about **24
  hours**. After 4 hours without power, throw away perishable food from the fridge (meat, fish,
  eggs, milk, leftovers). **When in doubt, throw it out.**
- Eat the fridge first, then the freezer, then the cupboard.
- Never cook with a grill, camp stove or charcoal **indoors** — carbon monoxide.

**Together:** when the power is out for a long time, a shared meal from everyone's thawing
freezers wastes nothing. Long term, community gardens and shared stores do more than private
stockpiles (Hesperian, *Community food security*, in the library).

Sources: CDC, *Discarding Food After a Power Outage*
(cdc.gov/natural-disasters/psa-toolkit/discarding-food-after-a-power-outage.html);
FoodSafety.gov, *Three ways to keep food safe when you lose power*.

## Electricity
<!-- id: energy -->

**Know what you need.** Write down what really needs power: phones and a radio, lights, a
fridge with medicines, medical equipment, a router for the local network, a laptop. Everything
else waits.

**Power banks and batteries.** A power bank's capacity in watt-hours (Wh) = mAh × volts ÷ 1000.
A "10,000 mAh" bank (at 3.7 V) holds about 37 Wh; a phone battery holds roughly 12–20 Wh, so
expect about two phone charges after losses. Keep banks charged and rotate spare batteries.

**Solar.** A small folding panel charges phones and power banks in direct sun; expect much
less than the number on its label, and nothing under clouds. Charge a power bank during the
day, and charge phones from the bank at night.

**The car.** A car's 12 V socket can charge phones — only with the **engine running outdoors**
and never in a closed garage (carbon monoxide). Do not run the starter battery flat.

**Generators — carbon monoxide kills without warning:**

- **Only outdoors**, at least **6 metres (20 feet)** from the house, with the exhaust pointing
  away from doors, windows and vents. **Never** inside a home, garage, basement or shed — not
  even with doors and windows open.
- Have a **battery carbon-monoxide alarm** near where people sleep.
- **Never plug a generator into a wall socket**: it can send power back into the lines and
  start fires. Plug appliances into it with heavy-duty outdoor extension cords.
- Turn it off and let it cool before refuelling; keep fuel away from it; do not use it in rain.

**Candles** can start fires; prefer battery lights.

**Together:** one generator or solar kit can keep a whole building's phones, a radio and a
medicine fridge going if people share it on a schedule. Put the charging point in a common
place and write the turns on paper. Check on neighbours who run generators.

Sources: CDC, *When the power goes out, keep your generator outside*;
US CPSC, *What to Know About Generators and Carbon Monoxide*;
FEMA generator safety guidance (in the library).

## Staying connected
<!-- id: connect -->

**When phones still work but the network is overloaded:** send **text messages**, keep calls
short, and agree on **one person outside the area** that everyone reports to.

**Radio.** A battery or hand-crank AM/FM radio is the most reliable way to hear official news.

**Without internet, your own local network still works.** A Wi-Fi router powered from a power
bank or a 12 V battery makes a small network with no internet. Datas' devices link over it
(Devices → link with a code): phones can open the Datas of a laptop, chat with its AI and read
this library — nothing leaves the building.

**Keep things offline before you need them:**

- maps saved for offline use in a map app (many are built on OpenStreetMap);
- this library, and a small AI in *My AIs*;
- a whole offline encyclopedia: **Kiwix** (kiwix.org) keeps Wikipedia, the medical WikiMed,
  Wikivoyage and more on a phone or computer.

**Text over radio with no phone network: Meshtastic** (meshtastic.org) — open-source, low-cost
LoRa radios that pass short encrypted messages from radio to radio over kilometres, with no
phone network or internet. In many countries these bands need no licence, but you must choose
the right region and follow its rules. It works best when many neighbours have one.

**Amateur ("ham") radio** reaches much farther and has organised emergency networks, but it
needs a licence in almost every country — get it before the emergency.

**Together:** a **phone tree** (each person calls or messages two others), a paper message board
at the meeting point, and one radio per block that someone listens to at fixed hours.

Sources: Meshtastic documentation (meshtastic.org/docs); Kiwix (kiwix.org).

## Health and first aid
<!-- id: health -->

- Learn **first aid** before you need it; the library has the first-aid chapter of
  *Where There Is No Doctor* (English and Spanish).
- Keep a **list of each person's conditions, allergies and medicines** on paper, and a supply of
  the medicines you take every day. Medicines that need cold (like insulin) are a priority for
  any fridge or generator.
- **Clean water, soap and safe toilets** prevent most sickness after a disaster.
- Watch for **dehydration**, **cold** (shivering, confusion) and **heat**, especially in
  children and older people.
- This app is not a doctor. When emergency services can be reached, call them.

**Together:** find out who in your area knows first aid or works in health, and where the
nearest health post is. Older and sick people need someone who checks on them every day.

Sources: Hesperian, *Where There Is No Doctor* / *Donde no hay doctor* (library).

## Outdoors: shelter, warmth and finding your way
<!-- id: outdoors -->

- **Stay dry and out of the wind**; insulate from the ground — the cold ground takes heat fast.
- Wear **layers**; wool keeps warm when damp, cotton does not.
- In a car stuck in snow, stay with the car, keep the exhaust pipe clear of snow and run the
  engine only for short periods with a window slightly open.
- **Finding your way**: carry a paper map and a compass and learn to use them; tell someone
  your route and when you will be back.
- **Signalling**: three of anything (whistles, fires, flashes) is a call for help.

The library has *The Book of Camp-Lore and Woodcraft* for fire, shelter, knots and finding your
way.

**Together:** a group moves at the pace of its slowest member and never leaves anyone behind.

## A crisis while travelling
<!-- id: travel -->

- Before leaving: **copies of passport and documents** on paper and offline on the phone;
  emergency contacts written down; a power bank; some cash in small notes; maps saved offline.
- Know how to reach **your country's embassy or consulate** where you go.
- Agree with the people you travel with on a **meeting point** and on what each does if you are
  separated.
- If transport stops: stay where it is safe, follow local authorities, and let one person at
  home know where you are.

**Together:** other travellers and local people are your community for those days — share
information, chargers and space.

## Living on your own means for a long time
<!-- id: longterm -->

When normal supplies stop for weeks or months, everything becomes a cycle:

- **Water**: collect rain from roofs into clean covered tanks, keep sources protected, treat what
  you drink, plan toilets so they never pollute water.
- **Food**: grow what grows easily where you are, keep seeds, preserve harvests (drying, canning
  done correctly), rotate stores.
- **Energy**: solar panels with batteries for lights, phones, a radio and a fridge; wood or gas
  for cooking used safely.
- **Health**: hygiene, prevention, first aid, and a shared medicine kit.
- **Skills over stuff**: repairing, sewing, cooking from basics, teaching children.
- **Knowledge**: keep this library, a small AI and an offline encyclopedia on a device you can
  charge with the sun.

**Together:** long crises are survived by groups that share work, decide fairly and look after
the weakest. Kropotkin's *Mutual Aid* (library) is the classic case that cooperation — not only
competition — keeps groups alive.

Sources: Hesperian, *A Community Guide to Environmental Health* (library);
WHO/WEDC technical notes (library).

## Doing it together
<!-- id: community -->

The people near you are the first and often the only help for days. Before anything happens:

1. **Map your street or building**: who lives where, who lives alone, who has children, older
   people, a disability, a medicine that needs power or cold, pets.
2. **List skills and things**: who knows first aid, electricity, plumbing, cooking for many,
   languages; who has a generator, a solar panel, a water tank, tools, a car, a radio.
3. **Agree a meeting point** and a **paper message board** there.
4. **Make a phone tree** and one contact outside the area for everyone.
5. **Share, do not hoard**: shared water, shared charging, shared meals. A neighbour who is
   helped today helps tomorrow.
6. **Decide openly** who does what, and change roles so nobody burns out.
7. **Practise once a year** — a short drill makes the real thing calmer.

Many countries train neighbours as **Community Emergency Response Teams** (CERT); FEMA's CERT
manual in the library shows how such teams organise, put out small fires, search lightly and
help the injured until professionals arrive.

**With Datas:** one laptop with a small AI and this library, kept charged by a solar panel or
a power bank, can serve a whole building over a local Wi-Fi network: everyone's phone can link
to it and ask.

Sources: FEMA, *CERT Basic Training Participant Manual*;
FEMA, *Are You Ready?*;
FEMA / Ready.gov, *Disaster Preparedness Guide for Older Adults* (all in the library).

## Using Datas when the internet is gone
<!-- id: datas -->

- **Get it ready now, while there is internet**: download this library (*Keep everything*), and a
  small AI in *My AIs* — the smallest one that runs on your device is enough to look things up
  here.
- **Ask the survival helper** (*Ask*): it answers from these guides and the documents you kept,
  and says which document it used. It works with no internet.
- **Read without the AI**: every guide and document opens directly, with no AI at all, which also
  saves battery.
- **Save battery**: close Datas' AI when you are not asking (*My AIs → Stop*); a phone or a small
  laptop uses far less power than a desktop.
- **Share it**: link your phones and other computers (Devices) over a local Wi-Fi network; they
  can use this computer's AI and library with no internet.
